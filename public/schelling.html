Schelling's Model of Segregation

Two kinds of agent — red and blue — are scattered on a grid with some empty cells. Each agent is happy only if at least a threshold share of its occupied neighbours are the same kind; an unhappy one packs up and moves to a random empty square. Set that bar as low as 35% — "I'd like a third of my neighbours to be like me" — and watch the whole grid congeal into large, sharply bordered enclaves. A tiny individual preference produces a huge collective pattern. Thomas Schelling won a Nobel for showing this in 1971; the grid is toroidal and a single seed drives every random choice, so a share link reproduces the exact same run.

What am I looking at?

Each dot is an agent that wants some of its neighbours to look like it. The slider is that demand, as a fraction of occupied neighbours. The model is deliberately colour-blind: red and blue play by the identical rule — there is no "red prefers red more than blue does." Yet after a few dozen rounds the grid separates into two big blocks with a crisp boundary. The boundary just keeps redrawing itself: every move is a single unhappy agent seeking comfort, and the aggregate is segregation nobody planned.

Read the avg same-neighbour number: start at ~50% (random mix) and watch it climb past 70–80% even at a 35% threshold. Lower the threshold toward 15% and the grid stays mostly integrated — the bias has to be only slightly above the "don't care" line to tip the system. That sensitivity is the model's enduring lesson. Stamp your own agents by dragging on the grid to see how a few foreign dots provoke local churn.
