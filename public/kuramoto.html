Kuramoto Oscillators

A population of N little clocks, each ticking at its own natural frequency ωi. Each clock is weakly nudged toward the average phase of the whole group, with coupling strength K. On its own, every clock just drifts at its own pace — a jumble. But once K is strong enough relative to the spread of frequencies Δω, the clocks spontaneously pull into step and a single giant rhythm emerges: collective synchronization, a continuous phase transition. The order parameter R = |(1/N)·Σ eiθ| climbs from ≈0 (everyone out of step) to ≈1 (locked in unison). The yellow arrow on the circle is that mean-field vector — its length is R. Below a critical coupling the crowd stays incoherent; above it, a beat appears out of nothing but local averaging. It's the temporal sibling of the Ising model's spatial transition — order in time, not space. Everything runs in your browser, driven by a single seed, so a share link reproduces the exact same run.

What am I looking at?

Each oscillator has a phase θi advancing as dθᵢ/dt = ωᵢ + (K/N)·Σⱼ sin(θⱼ − θᵢ). The sum can be collapsed into a single mean-field push toward the population average, so the whole thing stays an O(N) computation. When the spread of natural frequencies is wide and K is small, the coupling can't overcome the drift and the clocks stay scattered (R≈0). Raise K past the critical point Kc ≈ 2·Δω/π and the population snaps into a shared rhythm — a giant cluster in the phase distribution on the right. Pause it near the transition and you'll see the system flicker between locked and broken.

Set D > 0 to add thermal (Brownian) noise to each phase — it fights synchronization and you'll need more coupling to pull the clocks together. Pick Bimodal for two competing frequency tribes (a classic split-state case), Normal for a bell of frequencies, or Uniform for the textbook setup. The order parameter R and mean phase Ψ are computed live from the population — no hidden state.
